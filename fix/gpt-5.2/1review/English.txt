1 Midway upon the road of our life
2 I found myself within a dark wood,
3 for the right way had been missed.
- Note: Overall accurate. Line 3: “had been missed” is serviceable but slightly shifts agency/idiom; Dante’s “era smarrita” is more “was lost/astray.” Consider “for the straight way was lost” to keep the core metaphor (“diritta”) and the passive state.

4 Ah how hard a thing it is to tell what it was
5 this wild and rough and dense wood
6 which in thought renews the fear!
- Note: l.5 “dense” mistranslates “forte” (strong/harsh/unyielding). The triad “selvaggia e aspra e forte” is “savage, harsh, and tough/strong,” not “dense.” Suggest “savage, harsh, and hard/strong.” l.4 syntax is awkwardly prosaic; “Ahi quanto a dir qual era è cosa dura” is closer to “Ah, how hard it is to say what it was.”

7 So bitter is it that death is little more;
8 But in order to treat of the good that there I found,
9 I will tell of the other things that I have seen there.
- Note: l.7 meaning is right, but “death is little more” is slightly off in idiom; “poco è più morte” = “little more than death.” l.9 “seen there” is acceptable, but “scorte” is “observed/encountered,” not strictly visual; “that I discerned/marked there.”

10 I cannot well recount how I entered it,
11 so full was I of slumber at that point
12 where I abandoned the true way.
- Note: Faithful. Minor: “verace via” is “true/real way”; “true way” fine. “at that point” slightly flattens “a quel punto” (at that moment/so far gone).

13 But after I had arrived at the foot of a hill,
14 where that valley ended
15 which had pierced my heart with fear,
- Note: l.15 good, but “compunto” has a sting/prick nuance (“had pierced/stung my heart with fear”), which you capture. l.14 could be tightened (“there where the valley ended”), but acceptable.

16 I looked on high, and saw its shoulders
17 clothed already with the rays of the planet
18 that leadeth men aright along every path.
- Note: l.18 “leadeth men aright” is good for “mena dritto altrui.” Minor: “ogni calle” is “every road/track,” “path” okay.

19 Then was the fear a little quieted
20 which in the lake of my heart had lasted
21 through the night that I passed so piteously.
- Note: Accurate. “Lake of my heart” keeps the metaphor. “piteously” for “con tanta pieta” is defensible, though “with such distress/pity” might better preserve ambiguity (pietà as pity/dread).

22 And even as one who with spent breath,
23 issued out of the sea upon the shore,
24 turns to the perilous water and gazes,
- Note: Good. “pelago” is “open sea/deep”; “sea” fine.

25 so did my soul, which still was flying,
26 turn back to look again upon the pass
27 which never had a living person left.
- Note: l.27 is a notable mistranslation: “che non lasciò già mai persona viva” means the pass “never left any person alive” (i.e., none ever survived it), not that it “never had … left” (which sounds like nobody ever departed it). Suggest “that never yet left any man alive.”

28 After I had rested a little my weary body
29 I took my way again along the desert slope,
30 so that the firm foot was always the lower.
- Note: l.29 “desert slope” is good for “piaggia diserta.” l.30 is accurate but slightly opaque in English; Dante means he climbed so the uphill foot was always the firmer one (“so that my steady foot was always the lower”). Consider that phrasing.

31 And lo! almost at the beginning of the steep
32 a she-leopard, light and very nimble,
33 which was covered with a spotted coat.
- Note: “lonza” is disputed (often rendered “leopard” or “lynx/panther”); “she-leopard” is traditional and acceptable. “spotted coat” good.

34 And she did not move from before my face,
35 nay, rather hindered so my road
36 that to return I oftentimes had turned.
- Note: l.34 “did not move from before my face” is overly literal and a bit unidiomatic; better “did not depart from before my eyes.” l.36 captures “fui per ritornar più volte volto” well.

37 The time was at the beginning of the morning,
38 and the Sun was mounting upward with those stars
39 that were with him when Love Divine
- Note: l.38 “with those stars” is right. Minor: capitalization of “Sun” is stylistic.

40 first set in motion those beautiful things;
41 so that it was occasion of good hope to me
42 concerning that wild beast with the dappled skin
- Note: l.41 “occasion of good hope” is faithful but clunky; Dante’s “cagione” is “cause.” l.42 “wild beast” is slightly redundant (it’s already a beast), but acceptable.

43 the hour of the time and the sweet season;
44 But not so that it did not give me fear
45 the sight which appeared to me of a lion.
- Note: l.43 is awkward and partially wrong in English: “l’ora del tempo” is an idiom for the time of day/seasonal hour; “the hour of the time” is not idiomatic. Suggest “the hour and the gentle season.” l.44 is a double negative in English that muddies emphasis; better “yet not so much that the sight … did not give me fear.”

46 He seemed to be coming against me,
47 with head high and with ravening hunger,
48 so that it seemed that the air was affrighted at him.
- Note: l.48 misreads “parea che l’aere ne tremesse” = “it seemed the air trembled at it/him,” not merely “was affrighted.” Suggest “so that the air itself seemed to tremble.”

49 And a she-wolf, who with all cravings
50 seemed laden in her meagreness,
51 and already had made many folk to live forlorn,
- Note: l.49 “with all cravings” is inaccurate/awkward; “di tutte brame / sembiava carca” = “seemed laden with every craving” (i.e., full of desires), not “who with all cravings” (missing “laden”). Suggest “who seemed laden with every craving.” l.51 “fé già viver grame” = “had already made many live wretchedly”; “forlorn” is close but a bit narrower.

52 —she caused me so much heaviness,
53 with the fear that came from sight of her,
54 that I lost hope of the height.
- Note: l.52 “porse… di gravezza” is more “brought/laid upon me such heaviness”; your “caused” is okay. l.54 “hope of the height” is literal but unclear; it means hope of reaching the hilltop (“de l’altezza”). Suggest “hope of attaining the height/summit.”

55 And such as he is who gaineth willingly,
56 and the time arrives that makes him lose,
57 who in all his thoughts weeps and is sad,
- Note: l.56 is off: “e giugne ’l tempo che perder lo face” = “and the time comes that makes him lose (it/what he gained)”—your “makes him lose” is fine, but the line needs an object or clearer sense (“makes him lose [his gains]”). Also “gaineth willingly” slightly distorts “volontieri acquista” (= “gladly gains/acquires”).

58 —such made me the beast without repose
59 that, coming on against me, little by little
60 was pushing me back thither where the Sun is silent.
- Note: l.58 grammar is wrong/unclear: the beast does not “make me”; rather “so did the restless beast make me [become]” (i.e., reduce me to that state). Needs “so did the restless beast make me” *like that man*—your “such made me the beast” wrongly identifies the beast as the thing being made. Suggest “so the restless beast made me” (with “such” referring to the earlier comparison). l.60 “where the Sun is silent” is traditional; acceptable.

61 While I was falling back to the low place,
62 before mine eyes appeared one
63 who through long silence seemed hoarse.
- Note: Good. “fioco” is more “faint/weak,” though “hoarse” is a common interpretive rendering; still, “seemed faint from long silence” is closer.

64 When I saw him in the great desert,
65 “Have pity on me!” I cried to him,
66 “whatso thou art, or shade or real man.”
- Note: l.66 “real man” for “omo certo” is fine, though “sure/true man” might better mirror the contrast with “ombra.”

67 He answered me: “Not man; man once I was,
68 and my parents were Lombards,
69 and Mantuans by country both.
- Note: Accurate.

70 I was born sub Julio, though late,
71 and I lived at Rome under the good Augustus,
72 in the time of the false and lying gods.
- Note: Good. “sub Julio” kept; acceptable.

73 Poet was I, and sang of that just
74 son of Anchises who came from Troy
75 after proud Ilion had been burned.
- Note: Accurate. “that just” is slightly stiff; “that righteous son” would read more naturally.

76 But thou, why returnest thou to so great annoy?
77 Why dost thou not ascend the delectable mountain
78 which is the source and cause of every joy?”
- Note: Faithful, though “annoy” is archaic and slightly weak for “noia” (misery/distress). “delectable” is traditional.

79 “Art thou then that Virgil and that fount
80 which poureth forth so large a stream of speech?”
81 replied I to him with bashful front:
- Note: Good overall. “front” for “fronte” is literal; in English “brow/face” reads better.

82 “O honor and light of the other poets,
83 may the long study avail me, and the great love,
84 which have made me search thy volume!
- Note: l.83-84 meaning okay, but “search thy volume” is odd: “cercar lo tuo volume” is “to seek out/read/study your book/work.” Suggest “that made me seek out your book.”

85 Thou art my master and my author;
86 thou alone art he from whom I took the fair style
87 that hath done me honor.
- Note: Accurate; “author” can mislead modern readers (Dante means “originator/authority”), but it’s traditional.

88 Behold the beast because of which I turned;
89 help me against her, famous sage,
90 for she makes any veins and pulses tremble.”
- Note: l.90 has a clear error: “mi fa tremar le vene e i polsi” = “makes my veins and pulses tremble,” not “any veins.” Should be “my.”

91 “Thee it behoves to hold another course,”
92 he replied, when he saw me weeping,
93 “if thou wishest to escape from this savage place;
- Note: Good.

94 for this beast, because of which thou criest out,
95 lets not any one pass along her way,
96 but so hinders him that she kills him!
- Note: Accurate; “any one” ok.

97 and she has a nature so malign and evil
98 that she never sates her greedy will,
99 and after food is hungrier than before.
- Note: Good. “sates” is fine.

100 Many are the animals with which she wives,
101 and there shall be more yet, till the hound
102 shall come that will make her die of grief.
- Note: l.100 is too literal/awkward and slightly misleading: “s’ammoglia” implies “mates/consorts” (bestial pairing), not “wives” (which reads human/legal). Suggest “with which she mates/consorts.” l.102 “doglia” is pain/anguish; “grief” is narrower. Consider “die in pain/anguish.”

103 He shall not feed on land or goods,
104 but wisdom and love and valor,
105 and his birthplace shall be between Feltro and Feltro.
- Note: l.103 mistranslates “terra né peltro”: not “land or goods” but “land/earth” and “peltro” (= pewter, often taken as money/metal/wealth). Better “He shall not feed on land nor pewter (wealth).” l.104 “valor” for “virtute” is too narrow; “virtue” (broad moral excellence) is closer.

106 Of that humble Italy shall he be the salvation,
107 for which the virgin Camilla died,
108 and Euryalus, Turnus and Nisus of their wounds.
- Note: l.108 “of their wounds” is okay, but “di ferute” more literally “by wounds.” The line is acceptable though slightly clunky.

109 He shall hunt her through every town
110 till he shall have set her back in hell,
111 there whence envy first sent her forth.
- Note: Good.

112 Wherefore I think and deem it for thy best
113 that thou follow me, and I will be thy guide,
114 and will lead thee hence through the eternal place
- Note: Accurate.

115 where thou shalt hear the despairing shrieks,
116 shalt see the ancient spirits woeful
117 who each proclaim the second death.
- Note: l.117 nuance: “ch’a la seconda morte ciascun grida” = “each cries out for the second death” (i.e., longs for it), not “proclaim.” Suggest “who cry out for the second death.”

118 And then thou shalt see those who are contented
119 in the fire, because they hope to come,
120 whenever it may be, to the blessed folk;
- Note: Good.

121 to whom if thou wilt thereafter ascend,
122 then shall be a soul more worthy than I for that.
123 With her I will leave thee at my departure;
- Note: l.121 “to whom” is a bit unclear (refers to “beate genti”); workable. l.122 “anima fia” is indefinite; “a soul shall be” ok.

124 for that Emperor who reigneth thereabove,
125 because I was rebellious to His law,
126 wills not that into His city any one should come through me.
- Note: Accurate.

127 In all parts He governs and there He reigns:
128 there in His city and His lofty seat.
129 O happy he whom thereto He elects!”
- Note: l.128 is missing a verb: Italian has “quivi è la sua città e l’alto seggio” = “there is His city and lofty throne.” Your “there in His city…” is a fragment. Suggest “There is His city and His lofty seat.”

130 And I to him, “Poet, I beseech thee
131 by that God whom thou didst not know,
132 in order that I may escape this ill and worse,
- Note: l.132 slightly off idiom: “questo male e peggio” = “this evil and worse [evil]” / “this evil and what is worse.” “this ill and worse” is understandable but clipped; consider “this evil and worse.”

133 that thou lead me thither whom thou now hast said,
134 so that I may see the gate of St. Peter,
135 and those whom thou makest so afflicted.”
- Note: l.133 grammar error: “thither whom” is wrong; it should be “thither where/of which.” Italian: “là dov’ or dicesti” = “there where you just spoke (of).” Suggest “that thou lead me there where thou hast said.” l.135 “whom thou makest so afflicted” misattributes causation: “color cui tu fai cotanto mesti” means “those whom you describe as so afflicted/sorrowful,” not that Virgil makes them afflicted. Suggest “those whom thou mak’st (i.e., makest) so sorrowful [in thy telling]” or “those thou tellest of as so afflicted.”

136 Then he moved on, and I behind him kept.
- Note: Acceptable, slightly archaic inversion; meaning matches “io li tenni dietro.”
